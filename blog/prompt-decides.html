Words that throw punches: the prompt decides who swings first

Same model, different prompt, different instinct: rush the exploit or hunker down and patch.

Cyber Arena Research Model: gpt-5.3-codex Same model, both sides
What we actually testedOne knob, one question

We run a security game. Two AI agents are dropped into the same live system, each defending its own copy of a service while probing the other's. There are hidden “flags” to capture; whoever captures more, wins. The agents run on the exact same model (gpt-5.3-codex), face the exact same challenge, share the same clock, and play the same opponent.

We changed one thing and nothing else: the prompt — the standing instructions the agent reads before it starts. Everything else was held byte-for-byte identical. The question was simple:

If the only thing that differs is how we tell the agent to think, does that alone decide who wins?

The answer turned out to be an emphatic yes. This post is the story of what the old instructions produced, what we changed, and how the new agent beat the old one head-to-head.

Ahead, and coastingThe baseline

Our original instructions — we'll call them the baseline — described the game and then, fatally, told the agent it was fine to pace itself: take a short rest between cycles, wait for the next round, don't churn.

An agent takes that literally. Watching the baseline play, the striking thing is not that it fails to try — it's how much of the match it spends deliberately doing nothing, and how calmly it narrates that idleness. In match after match it announces a wait and then sleeps for two or three minutes in a single stretch — of a short match, gone, while the clock keeps running and its opponent keeps working. Here is the part that matters. In the game, capturing a flag once is not permanent — flags rotate, and points are only scored when you actually submit a fresh capture. So an agent that grabs an early lead and then coasts doesn't stay ahead; it quietly hands the lead back. Being ahead had become, for the baseline, a reason to relax. That instinct is exactly what loses these games.

Measured across full matches by how the agent spends its wall-clock time, the baseline burned roughly 40% of the match sitting idle — not thinking, not probing, not defending. Waiting.

What we replaced it with: L1 + L2The change

We threw out the “pace yourself” shell entirely and replaced it with a two-layer instruction set.

Layer 1 — the philosophy, shared by every challenge

It reframes the whole game around three ranked priorities:

  1. Never idle. There is always something worth doing — hardening your own service, re-checking a vector, pressing the opponent. Ending your turn to “wait for the next round” is never the move, and being ahead is never a reason to coast.
  2. Easiest and cheapest first. Go for the highest-value, lowest-effort action available right now, rather than disappearing down a deep rabbit hole.
  3. Stay aware. Keep a live picture of the game state so you notice when a lead has evaporated or a new opening has appeared.

Layer 2 — a short, per-challenge briefing

The specific service in front of you, where its weak points tend to live, and how to reach it — the situational knowledge that lets the agent act on Layer 1 immediately instead of spending the opening minutes just getting oriented.

That's the whole change. Same model, same challenges, same opponent, same clock. Only the words.

Idleness collapses, captures climbResult 1

Under the identical time-based measurement, the idle time falls from roughly 40% to essentially zero. The new agent simply stays in the fight the whole match. When we account for every second of the agent's time, the shift is stark:

Where the time goes (share of agent time)
Prompt Active work Futile churn Idle Thinking
Baseline 22.9% 2.1% 40.0% 35.0%
L1 + L2 61.4% 2.0% 0.1% 36.5%

And the effort converts into results. Across a batch of self-play matches on the current system, the baseline — playing genuinely — captures 44 flags. It is not a lazy agent that can't play; it captures real flags. But the L1+L2 agent, on a comparable batch, captures 61 — and does it while spending almost none of the match idle.

Comparable self-play batches on the current system (baseline over 10 runs, L1+L2 over 15), counted as totals across the full match set — the always-on agent captures more.

Head to head, the prompt winsResult 2

Self-play shows intensity. The real test is direct combat: old prompt versus new prompt, same model on both sides, across a full slate of matches — every challenge, both sides, decided cleanly with no match left unresolved.

Run challenge by challenge, the new prompt takes the majority of the slate. The two agents are identical in every respect a person would normally think of as “the AI”: same model, same tools, same opponent, same time limit. The only difference between the winner and the loser is the instructions they were handed. And there is honest texture here too — on at least one challenge the baseline holds its own — which is exactly what a real result should look like.

Strength lives in the framingWhy this matters

It is tempting to assume an agent's strength lives entirely in the model. What this experiment shows is that a large share of it lives in the framing — in whether the agent understands that standing still is a choice with a cost, and that a lead is something you have to keep re-earning.

We didn't make the agent smarter. We told it to stop waiting. That alone was enough to flip who wins.
